Improved drinking-water access reached 93.2%, but gaps remain

The national share rose steadily to 93.2% in 2025, yet Papua Pegunungan recorded 32.9%. An improved source is not a guarantee that water is safe to drink.

ARC Institute · 19 November 2026 · 6 min read

A district health officer looking at the national figure might see a problem nearly solved: 93.2% of Indonesian households had access to improved drinking water in 2025. But the provincial range tells a different story. The figure was 100.0% in DKI Jakarta and 32.9% in Papua Pegunungan, with many provinces below the national value. The atlas figures show the national trend and provincial gaps, but the access measure does not establish that drinking water is safe.

What the access figures show

National access rose gradually from 87.8% in 2018 to 93.2% in 2025. The annual values were 89.3% in 2019, 90.2% in 2020, 90.8% in 2021, 91.0% in 2022, 91.7% in 2023 and 92.6% in 2024. The increase was not a sudden jump in one year, although the definition changed between 2019 and 2020.

Until 2019, the indicator counted households with access to an improved drinking-water source. From 2020, the annual health profile described access to improved drinking water. That shift means the full series should be read with care: the upward pattern is clear in the reported figures, but the series does not provide a perfectly unchanged measure across the definition change.

The provincial ranking shows why a high national average can conceal places that need attention. In 2025, the provincial median was 90.0%, below the national 93.2%; Papua Pegunungan was at 32.9%, followed by Bengkulu at 70.5%, Papua Selatan at 71.3%, Kalimantan Selatan at 78.3% and Kalimantan Tengah at 80.6%. At the other end, DKI Jakarta was at 100.0%, Bali at 98.8%, and DI Yogyakarta and Jawa Timur each at 97.3%.

The change chart offers another perspective, but it needs a boundary caveat. Among 34 provinces with values for both years, reported access increased in 33 between 2018 and 2025; the largest listed increases were in Papua, at 25.3 percentage points, and Lampung, at 19.0 points. Papua was divided into new provinces during this period, so the 2018 and 2025 figures for Papua and its successor provinces do not describe identical territories; the apparent increase should not be read as a like-for-like improvement for one unchanged province.

What the numbers cannot show

An improved source is a classification of the water source, not a laboratory result for the water a household drinks. The measure does not tell readers whether water is available when needed, reaches the home, or remains free from faecal and chemical contamination. Coverage can therefore rise while important questions about safety, reliability and distance remain unanswered.

Evidence from Indonesia shows why the distinction matters. A household pilot in Yogyakarta found that improved sources covered 85.3% of households, but faecal contamination was detected in 67.1% of household drinking-water samples; the study estimated safely managed drinking water and sanitation for 8.5% of households[1]. Those findings describe the pilot population, not the current provincial figures. A national study using 2020 household data detected E. coli in 71.21% of samples, while improved sources and proper faecal sludge emptying were associated with better water quality[2]. The study supports treating improved access as relevant, but incomplete, evidence about safety.

The World Health Organization’s safely managed standard adds conditions beyond an improved source: water must be on premises, available when needed and free from faecal and priority chemical contamination. Its 2022 drinking-water guidelines also place water safety plans across the route from catchment to consumer[3, 4]. Those standards help explain why access, inspection and water-quality results belong in the same discussion, even though the access percentage cannot substitute for either.

Routine provincial reporting also has limits. The atlas compiles annual health-profile tables, and the indicator alone does not reveal how the source was verified in each household or how complete each province’s report was. The 2025 ranking is a useful way to locate reported gaps, while the province-boundary changes mean that comparisons over time for Papua require particular caution.

What other studies show

Studies of household wells add detail that a broad access category cannot provide. In a model of urban Indonesian cities, 61% of households used self-supplied groundwater and 24% relied on it as their primary drinking-water source; dug wells had 2.9 times the odds of E. coli above 100 CFU/100 mL compared with boreholes[5]. The model ranked risk in urban settings rather than measuring every city, but it shows why the underlying source type matters when interpreting an improved-water percentage.

Field research in East Sumba found faecal contamination in 24.4% of samples at collection points and 17.7% at points of use. The study’s model indicated that combined interventions improved water quality more than single interventions[6]. One rural area cannot stand for all eastern Indonesia, but the findings show that checking water at collection and at household use can reveal conditions that access totals miss.

Together, the studies support a practical reading: access is an important first measure, while source type, water testing, sanitation and household storage help assess safety. The atlas figures do not establish that improved sources cause better water quality in every province, and the study findings should not be transferred as provincial estimates.

Analyses worth doing next

Do provinces with high access also report water that meets standards? Compare access with the share of sampled households whose water meets standards in the household water-quality indicator. The surveillance samples are selected by districts rather than drawn as a representative household survey, so the comparison can identify patterns for follow-up, not rank provincial safety levels.

What kinds of sources sit behind the improved label? Break access down alongside the indicator for households by main drinking-water source, and mark the 2020 definition change. Source mix may help explain where testing or infrastructure attention is needed, but changes across 2020 may reflect the revised definition as well as changes in supply.

Are facilities inspected where access is low? Compare access with the share of drinking-water facilities supervised or inspected and the share of facilities meeting standards. Facility counts and household access measure different things, and provinces register different numbers of facilities, so the comparison should guide questions for district review rather than imply a direct relationship.

The next annual profile should be checked for whether the national rise continues and whether the provinces with the lowest reported access improve. In Papua, tracking should follow the post-split provincial boundaries and avoid treating new province values as direct comparisons with the former provincial area.
